## 1. In one sentence

> **PyPlym AI Edition is Plymouth's free monthly community where people learn to use AI from the human side and build with it honestly: developers and non-technical people, side by side.**

## 2. Why this is a good idea

1. **AI is in everyone's work now, and it can help or harm.** Students who practised maths with unrestricted ChatGPT scored 17% lower in the exam than those without it; a tutor version that gave hints instead of answers largely removed the harm ([Bastani et al., PNAS 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html)) [V]. Among 319 knowledge workers, **more confidence in AI went with less critical thinking**, while more self-confidence went with more ([Lee et al., CHI 2025](https://advait.org/files/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf)) [V]. How people use AI matters as much as which tool they use.
2. **Most AI events show tools.** Few explain how people learn, decide or delegate, or how the technology fails. Fewer let people build something.
3. **Developers and non-technical people rarely learn together**, yet they need each other: one understands the work, the other the technology.
4. **Plymouth is investing in technology and skills** (the £50m Defence Growth Deal, the National Centre for Marine Autonomy, new technical employers such as Helsing's maritime factory) [V], and needs people who can apply AI well, not only build it.
5. **The organiser has done this before.** PyPlym previously produced professional connections and job offers [F]. **One participant went from taxi driver to developer, and is now a tech lead**; the story travelled far enough that Monument AI's Sam Nixson had heard it before meeting the founder [F; use on slides only with that person's permission].

**The series spine: Human × Machine.** Every event pairs a human capability with its AI counterpart.

| Human side | Machine side |
| --- | --- |
| How we **learn** | How AI can tutor, and how it becomes a crutch |
| How we **delegate** | Agents: what can safely be handed over |

| How we **judge** what is true | Evaluating AI output; hallucination |
| How we **act** on goals | Agents that act; autonomy and accountability |
| How we **create together** | Building with AI as a team |

## 5. What each evening gives a participant

- **Understanding** of one human mechanism and one machine mechanism, from people who know both.
- **Something built**: a working prompt, tool or small script for their own real task.
- **A method** they can reuse the next day.
- **A connection**: a partner from the other side of the technical divide.
- **Open materials** afterwards, and a place to share results at the next event.

## 6. Structure

### An evening (about two and a half hours) [P]

| Time | Block | Led by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 18:00 | Arrive, food, find your pair | Operations |
| 18:20 | **The human side** (10–20 min): what the research says about the topic | Cecy |
| 18:40 | **The machine side** (20 min): how the technology works, and where it goes wrong | Tomasz |
| 19:00 | **Build in pairs** (60 min): a developer and a non-technical person work on the non-technical person's real task | Everyone; Tomasz and helpers circulate |
| 20:00 | **Show and tell** (20 min): what worked, what did not | Pairs |
| 20:20 | Close: what to try before next time | Tomasz |

### How pairing works [P]

- At registration, people choose **builder** (comfortable with code) or **explorer** (bring a real task, no coding needed).
- **The explorer drives; the builder navigates.** Borrowed from pair programming: the person with the problem keeps their hands on the keyboard, so they leave able to do it again.
- Uneven numbers become trios; explorers can also pair with each other on no-code tasks.
- **Keep the technical core.** Explorers make up **at most about half** of places, so every explorer has a builder; extra explorers join a waitlist. The machine-side block keeps real technical depth. (Lesson from the founder's observation: when a local event became non-technical, technical people stopped coming.)
- **Filter for curiosity, not coding.** Explorers are people willing to try something technical with help; the registration page says so plainly.
- Explorers are asked to bring one real task from their work or life.

## 7. The first two events in detail

### 7.1 Event 1: Learn better with AI, without letting it think for you

**Human side (Cecy).** What research on learning shows, presented with sources:
- People remember more when they **retrieve** information (testing themselves) than when they reread it, and when practice is **spaced** over time [K: classic learning-science findings, for example Roediger and Karpicke 2006; Cepeda et al. 2006].
- Effortful learning feels slower but lasts longer ("desirable difficulties") [K: Bjork].
- **The crutch effect:** unrestricted AI help raised practice scores but cut exam scores by 17%; hint-based tutoring avoided the harm (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025) [V]. **What the tutor actually was:** hint instructions **plus teacher-written correct solutions and common mistakes for each problem**, in Turkish high-school maths; it removed the harm but did not beat the no-AI group, and 17% is a relative drop. A tutor prompt written in an evening is a different intervention; build the pairs' assistant the same way (give it worked solutions and typical mistakes), and check what people can do unaided afterwards.
- **Confidence and thinking:** trusting AI more went with thinking critically less (Lee et al., CHI 2025) [V].

**Machine side (Tomasz).**
- Why language models produce **fluent, plausible** answers that are not necessarily correct.
- Pitfalls for learners: confident errors, agreeing with the user, answers instead of understanding, limits of what the model can see.
- How to turn AI into a **tutor**: ask it to quiz you, give hints not answers, make you explain back, space your practice.

**Build in pairs.** The explorer picks something they want to learn. The pair builds a **personal learning assistant**: a tutor prompt that quizzes and hints, or a small script that turns notes into spaced questions. Each pair commits to using it for two weeks and reporting back at event 2.

**Takeaway:** *use AI to make you do the thinking, not to do it for you.*

### 7.2 Event 2: Delegate well, to people and to AI

**Human side (Cecy).**
- Delegation means **giving up control** and carrying the risk; people tend to **under-delegate** to other people [unsourced]. *Check (17 Oct): the only* Computers in Human Behavior *paper found is the Raveendhran paper below, and it says the reluctance is specific to AI, not delegation in general. Find a separate source for under-delegation to people, or drop this bullet.*
- Leaders resist handing employee conversations to AI mainly because of the **expectation to show care**, most strongly for sensitive issues ([Raveendhran, Jago, Gratch and Fast, *Computers in Human Behavior* 186 (2027) 109139, online July 2026](https://www.darden.virginia.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/RaveendhranJagoGratchFast_2027.pdf)) [V, peer-reviewed; four preregistered studies of leaders and employee communication]. Its Study 3 finds the reluctance is **specific to AI**, not a general reluctance to delegate communication.
- **Accepting that AI is capable is not the same as being willing to let it act**, especially where judgement or empathy is involved ([AI & Society, 2026](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/42327/3/s00146-026-02858-5.pdf)) [V, secondary summary].
- Where the psychological tension sits: control, accountability, trust, and the feeling of "it's quicker to do it myself".

**Machine side (Tomasz).**
- What makes a task **delegable**: in one study, trust was the factor most associated with people's preferences, and people preferred designs where a human leads and the machine assists ([Lubars and Tan, NeurIPS 2019](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2019/poster/14480)) [V].
- How to brief an AI agent: goal, constraints, examples, and **a check step** before anything is acted on.
- **Over-reliance**: why people stop checking automated output, and how to design against it.

**Build in pairs.** The explorer brings one real recurring task. The pair maps it (what to keep, what to hand over, what to check) and builds a **delegated workflow with a human check**.

**Takeaway:** *delegate the task, keep the judgement.*

### Sponsor principles

- **PyPlym is a local community initiative, not a recruitment channel.** Sponsors support it as local organisations investing in practical AI skills.
- **Sponsors support practice; they do not set content.**
- **No product pitches or recruitment drives.** A short slot about how they use AI in their work is welcome.
- **A mix of sponsors.** At least one non-defence sponsor or in-kind partner alongside any defence sponsor; content stays away from weapons and surveillance applications.
- **Participants' details are never shared.** Contact happens only if a participant chooses it.
- **Sponsors are listed openly.**
